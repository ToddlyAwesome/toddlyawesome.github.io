Campaign Design / Print / Brand

DPRR
Campaign

Developing colors, icons, and campaign systems for the U.S. Army directorate focused on the mental health and wellbeing of soldiers, civilians, spouses, and families.

Scroll

DPRR is a U.S. Army directorate focused on the mental health and wellbeing of soldiers, civilians, spouses, and families. The goal was to create branding that provides energy and optimism while offering a sense of belonging — colors that are vibrant but never over-saturated, and icons that capture the season and message of each campaign.

01 — The Brief

Materials that give
leaders something
to work with.

DPRR materials provide an outline for public affairs officers and stakeholders to lead their own campaigns — bringing attention to important topics and resources for their soldiers, units, and families.

My role was to develop a cohesive visual system: colors, icons, templates, and campaign assets that could be deployed consistently across dozens of touchpoints.

02 — Design Approach

Energy and optimism.
A sense of belonging.

I developed colors and icons to capture the season of each campaign and its overall message. The palette ranges from bright to dark without becoming over-saturated — you can see this philosophy in the gradient icon at the heart of the logo system.

Visual consistency was key. Branding guidelines were applied across every material to ensure a cohesive identity for DPRR's outreach — from posters to digital content to stakeholder templates.

03 — Audience Variations

Designed for the
people carrying it out.

Partnering with military leaders, content experts, and stakeholders meant every design decision had to hold up under real-world scrutiny. The audience isn't passive — they're public affairs officers who need tools they can actually use.

Each variation maintains the typographic system and color language while shifting context — from the field to the family room — so the campaign speaks to every corner of Army life.

Soldiers — Huddle
Family — Community

Impact

Good design in this space isn't about looking good. It's about giving people something to hold onto — a visual system that public affairs officers, unit commanders, and families can deploy with confidence when it matters most.
